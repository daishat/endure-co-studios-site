WORK, LIFE & PERSONAL REFLECTION

Why Is It So Damn Hard to Get a Job Right Now?

By D’Aisha · September 26, 2026

Across my social media feeds, all I keep seeing is people talking about how hard it is to get a job. Not just a job, either. A job that actually pays the bills, gives you some type of stability, and still gives you a chance to live your life. I don’t think most of us are asking to become millionaires from our 9-to-5. We just don’t want to work all the damn time and still live paycheck to paycheck.

Personally, I have put in over 400 applications in about six months, and out of those applications, I’ve had around 20 interviews. For six of those scheduled interviews, the interviewer either didn’t show up or arrived late. For other interviews, I was told I had the skills and seemed like a great candidate. I walked away thinking, “Okay, maybe this is finally it,” only to get an email saying they had decided to move forward with someone else. One job put me through three interviews, and I still didn’t get the offer.

After a while, you start wondering: What the hell am I doing wrong? I’ve changed my résumé. I’ve followed interview advice. I’ve applied locally and remotely. I’ve used local resources and looked into different industries. I’m also finishing a medical billing and coding course because I’m hoping that adding another skill and earning a certification will give me better odds of getting into a stable career. And I’m still looking.

I know I’m not the only one, though. That’s actually why I wanted to research this in the first place. Is finding a job actually harder right now, or does it just feel harder because I’m the one living through it?

Turns Out, I’m Not Imagining All of It

The numbers don’t say there are no jobs, and I think that’s important to point out. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, the unemployment rate was 4.1% in August 2026, with about 7 million people unemployed. Employers also added 162,000 nonfarm payroll jobs that month. At first glance, that doesn’t sound horrible. Source: BLS Employment Situation — August 2026.

Then I kept reading. About 1.9 million people had been unemployed for 27 weeks or longer, which represented 27% of all unemployed people. That’s the statistic that got my attention because 27 weeks is more than six months. Suddenly, my own six-month job search didn’t look like some weird experience that was only happening to me. There are literally millions of people who have been unemployed that long.

The unemployment rate doesn’t even capture everybody who wants a job. BLS reported 5.7 million people outside the labor force who said they wanted a job, although they weren’t counted as unemployed because they hadn’t actively searched during the previous four weeks or weren’t currently available for work. Within that group, 441,000 people were classified as discouraged workers, meaning they wanted work but weren’t currently looking because they believed there were no jobs available for them. Source: BLS Employment Situation — August 2026.

That last number bothered me because I understand how somebody gets there. There are only so many applications, automatic rejection emails, interviews that go nowhere, and days of hearing absolutely nothing you can take before opening another job board starts filling you with anxiety.

There Are Millions of Job Openings. So Why Doesn’t It Feel Like It?

This is where things get interesting. The Bureau of Labor Statistics’ July 2026 Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey reported approximately 7.3 million job openings, but there were about 5.1 million hires during the month. Source: BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover — July 2026.

I learned something important while researching those numbers, though. You can’t just subtract 5.1 million hires from 7.3 million openings and say, “See! Employers refused to fill 2.2 million jobs!” That’s not what those numbers mean. BLS measures job openings as positions open on the last business day of the month, while hires represent additions to payroll throughout the entire month. They’re related measurements, but they aren’t measuring exactly the same thing.

Still, when you look at the bigger picture, something has definitely changed. BLS looked back at the labor market following the pandemic and found that job openings reached a historic high of about 12.2 million in March 2022. By December 2023, they had fallen to 8.9 million. The ratio of unemployed people per job opening also increased from a historic low of about 0.5 in March 2022 to 0.7 by the end of 2023. Source: BLS — Job Openings and Hires Decline in 2023 as the Labor Market Cools. By July 2026, openings stood at about 7.3 million.

So yes, jobs exist. I’m not going to sit here and say nobody is hiring because the numbers don’t support that. But the labor market isn’t the same market people were experiencing a few years ago, either.

Everybody Is Applying

This was probably one of the least surprising things I found. StaffingHub, reporting on LinkedIn research, said that U.S. applications per open position had doubled since spring 2022. The same article reported that 65% of job seekers said finding a job had become harder. Source: StaffingHub — 66% of Recruiters Say Finding Talent Is Harder, Though Applications Have Doubled.

National University also describes the 2026 labor market as cooling but highly competitive. Its roundup of hiring statistics points to a strange problem: application volumes have increased while employers simultaneously report difficulty finding candidates with the particular skills and experience they’re looking for. Because National University’s article combines statistics from a lot of different sources, I wouldn’t treat every number in it the same way I would direct BLS data, but it does help show the bigger picture. Source: National University — 67 Hiring Statistics for 2026.

That’s such a weird job market when you think about it. Workers are basically screaming, “Please hire me!” while employers are screaming, “We can’t find anybody!” Meanwhile, hundreds of applications are sitting between the two.

So when I send an application and hear absolutely nothing, there could be a lot more happening than me simply not being qualified. That doesn’t mean every rejection is the employer’s fault. Sometimes another person genuinely has more experience. Sometimes my résumé could be better. Sometimes I’m applying for something that isn’t as close of a match as I thought. But the idea that the solution is simply to send more and more and more applications doesn’t seem particularly useful, either. I’ve already tried that. Four hundred applications later, I can confidently say I don’t want to do another hundred the exact same way.

Maybe the Strategy Has to Change

This part of my research was a little uncomfortable because it forced me to look at what I could change, too. Career-development guidance published by Yale School of Management says repeatedly applying without landing interviews can be a sign that it’s time to rethink the approach. The recommendations include narrowing your target companies, tailoring your résumé to individual positions, improving your LinkedIn presence, networking with people at companies you’re interested in, following up after applying, and considering adjacent roles or industries when your current approach isn’t getting results. Source: Yale School of Management — Why Your Job Applications Aren’t Landing Interviews—And How to Change That.

Basically, maybe I need to stop throwing résumés into the void and hoping somebody eventually grabs one. And I get it. It’s just frustrating because customizing applications takes time. Job hunting already feels like a full-time job that doesn’t fucking pay you. Still, if 100 rushed applications aren’t working, maybe fewer, stronger applications combined with actual human contact are worth trying.

That’s something I’m changing in my own search. Instead of only searching job boards, I’m starting to focus more on the companies themselves. Who is actually hiring? Who is the recruiter? Who runs the department? Can I connect with that person on LinkedIn? Can I email someone? Is there a way to get my résumé in front of an actual person instead of clicking “submit” and praying? I can’t promise that’s going to magically get me hired because I’m still testing it myself. But after 400 applications, I’m willing to admit the old strategy isn’t giving me the results I need.

Then There’s AI

Of course AI had to get involved. StaffingHub’s coverage of LinkedIn’s research reported that 93% of recruiters planned to expand their use of AI, while many job seekers were also planning to use AI tools during their searches. Source: StaffingHub’s coverage of LinkedIn hiring research.

I came across plenty of articles while researching this that made some pretty dramatic claims about applicant tracking systems automatically throwing away résumés, AI deciding who gets interviewed, ghost jobs, and exactly how much more likely somebody is to get hired through a referral. One of the LinkedIn articles I found discusses many of those issues from a career coach’s perspective. Further reading: Jessica Hernandez — Not Hearing Back From Job Applications? Here’s Why. Some of those claims were harder to verify with the same kind of primary evidence I could get from BLS, though, so I’m not going to pretend every scary statistic about ATS or AI is proven just because somebody put a percentage next to it.

What I can say is that technology is becoming a bigger part of recruiting while applicants are also using technology to apply. To me, that creates a really strange cycle. Employers receive more applications and use more technology to process them. Job seekers use technology to submit more applications, and employers end up with even more applications. Somewhere inside all of that is an actual human being who just wants a damn job.

Remote Work Feels Like the Golden Ticket You Can See but Can’t Reach

Remote work has been particularly frustrating for me because I’ve worked remotely for over four years. I’m not someone trying to convince an employer that I might be able to work from home. I’ve already done it successfully, and I’m still struggling to get another remote job. That’s what makes remote work feel like a golden ticket sitting right in front of your face that you can’t grab.

For parents, I completely understand why it’s attractive. Childcare is expensive. Children have school schedules. They get sick. They have appointments. They have therapies. They need to be picked up, and somebody has to do it. A job might technically be available, but that doesn’t automatically make it a job that works for your life.

You can tell somebody, “Just get a job,” but what happens when that job requires mandatory overtime and their child has to be picked up? What happens when the schedule changes every week? What happens when childcare takes so much of the paycheck that going to work barely improves the household financially? I especially feel for single parents because there isn’t another adult in the house to split those responsibilities with. We need jobs, but we also need jobs that allow people to be human beings outside of work.

I’m Trying to Change Careers Without Starting My Entire Life Over

One of the things I’ve decided to do during this mess is finish my medical billing and coding training. That decision actually became more interesting once I looked at the employment numbers. Health care employment continued trending upward in August 2026, adding about 13,000 jobs, although BLS noted that growth had slowed compared with the industry’s average monthly gain of 32,000 over the previous 12 months. Home health care and hospitals were among the areas adding jobs. Source: BLS Employment Situation — August 2026.

That doesn’t mean getting a medical billing or coding certification guarantees me a job. It doesn’t. That’s another distinction I’ve learned to make during this research: an industry adding jobs does not automatically mean every occupation inside that industry is easy to enter. Still, it makes me feel better about learning a skill connected to an industry that continues to employ a lot of people.

Maybe changing careers doesn’t always mean going back to school for another four years. Sometimes it might mean figuring out what you already know, what transfers into another field, and what smaller skill or certification could fill the gap. That’s what I’m trying to do.

Being Unemployed Doesn’t Stop the Bills

This might be the part that gets lost when we talk about unemployment like it’s just a statistic. Your mortgage doesn’t care that you’ve submitted 400 applications. The electric company doesn’t care that you made it to the final interview. Your child still needs things. Your car still needs gas. Food still has to go in the refrigerator. You can be doing everything people tell you to do and still be sitting there thinking, “How the hell am I going to pay for all of this?”

People will say, “Well, just get unemployment for a while,” but that’s not enough for everybody. Then you look for other assistance and run into income limits. One thing that has always frustrated me is seeing programs evaluate gross income when gross income isn’t what actually lands in your bank account. You don’t get to take your gross pay home. Taxes come out, insurance can come out, and then you have housing, electricity, transportation, childcare, food, debt, and everything else waiting for what’s left.

The stress follows you everywhere. Eventually, even opening another rejection email can feel exhausting. I think we need to talk about that part of job searching, too, because behind unemployment rates, job-opening numbers, and application statistics are actual people trying to figure out how they’re going to make it through another month.

So What Are We Supposed to Do?

After reading all of this, I wish I could tell you I discovered some secret trick. I didn’t. There’s no magic résumé, guaranteed certification, or special number of applications where the universe finally says, “Okay, you’ve suffered enough. Here’s your job.”

What I found instead was something less exciting but probably more useful: if what you’re doing isn’t working, don’t just do more of it. Change something. Target fewer companies and actually research them. Adjust your résumé to the position instead of sending the exact same document everywhere. Look at the skills employers repeatedly ask for and figure out whether you’re missing something you can realistically learn. Reach out to recruiters. Use LinkedIn for more than clicking Easy Apply. Talk to people. Look at adjacent industries. If your current field isn’t hiring, figure out where your existing skills transfer before deciding you need an entirely new education.

And sometimes, close the damn laptop and take a break. Because “keep going” doesn’t have to mean sending another 100 applications tonight. Sometimes keeping going means resting today so you can try again tomorrow. Sometimes it means admitting your strategy isn’t working, asking for help, learning something new, or doing deliveries, freelance work, baking, creating, or whatever else keeps some money coming through the door while you’re trying to figure out what comes next.

That’s where I am. I haven’t landed the magical career at the end of this article. I’m still applying. I’m still finishing my training. I’m still trying different strategies, and I’m still getting anxious when I think about how many more applications I may have to submit. But after researching the numbers, at least I know something I didn’t know before: this job market really has changed.

That doesn’t mean I have no control over what happens next. It means I need to stop measuring my worth by every automatic rejection that lands in my inbox. Four hundred applications taught me that simply applying harder isn’t necessarily the answer. So now I’m trying to apply smarter.

And I’ll let you know how that goes.
